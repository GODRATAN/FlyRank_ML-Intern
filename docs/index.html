Abstract

Content teams may have many pages to review but limited time to inspect them individually. This study asks whether a simple machine-learning model can help decide which content observations should be reviewed first when there is a concern about future search-performance decline.

The experiment uses the FlyRank internship warehouse release v20260703. March 2026 search-performance signals are used as the model inputs, while April 2026 is kept separate and used only to define the future outcome. An observation is labelled positive when its average daily GSC impressions in April are at least 20% lower than in March. Logistic Regression uses five March-only features to produce a ranking score, which is compared with the transparent Week-4 baseline.

On the client-grouped held-out test split, the model measured Precision@20 of 0.85 and Precision@50 of 0.82, compared with 0.50 and 0.52 for the baseline. These results show that the model concentrated more observations with the defined future outcome near the top of the review queue in this experiment.

The result should be read as evidence about prioritization, not as proof that a page will decline or that refreshing a page will improve search performance.

Model Precision@20 0.85 17 of top 20 met the defined outcome
Model Precision@50 0.82 41 of top 50 met the defined outcome
What this study actually does: the model does not decide what an editor should change. It produces an ordered review queue. A human still checks the page, search context, intent and business situation before deciding whether any action makes sense.

1. Introduction / Problem

The practical problem is straightforward: a content team can have thousands of pages and only a limited amount of time for detailed review. Looking at every page with equal attention is therefore unrealistic.

That changes the question we need to answer. Instead of asking “Which pages are bad?”, this study asks: “Which observations should be reviewed first?”

This is treated as a ranking problem. The system is not intended to replace an editor. It is intended to make the first pass more focused by placing observations that look more relevant near the top of a review queue.

Research question

Can a simple machine-learning model help rank content observations for human review when the goal is to identify observations with a higher measured risk of the future-decline outcome defined for this experiment?

Decision supported

The decision is where to spend review time first. A higher model score means that an observation is ranked more highly for inspection. It does not mean that the page definitely needs an edit, that the page will definitely decline, or that an edit will improve its future performance.

1 Observe March Use search-performance information available at the decision point.
2 Define April outcome Identify observations whose average daily impressions fell by at least 20%.
3 Rank observations Logistic Regression produces a score used to order the review queue.
4 Human review An editor checks the actual page, search intent and surrounding context.

2. Data

The study uses the FlyRank internship warehouse release v20260703, hosted in the gated FlyRank/internship-warehouse dataset. The main table is fact_content_daily_performance, where each observation is at the report date × client × content grain. The release contains approximately 78.8 million daily-performance rows covering 2025-01-27 through 2026-06-30.

Two separate time windows

March 2026 · What the model is allowed to see

March is the decision/feature window. Only information available in this period is used as a model input.

  • march_impressions — search exposure
  • march_clicks — observed clicks
  • march_ctr — click-through rate
  • march_avg_position — average search position
  • march_days_observed — amount of observed evidence

April 2026 · What happens later

April is used only to construct the future outcome. It is not supplied to the model as a feature.

An observation receives label 1 when its average daily GSC impressions in April are at least 20% lower than its average daily GSC impressions in March.

Why the separation matters

The model is supposed to make its ranking using information that would actually have been available at the decision point. If April information were allowed into the March feature set, the model could indirectly see the future it is supposed to predict. Keeping the windows separate is therefore part of the experiment design, not just a data-cleaning detail.

Eligibility and exclusions

  • At least 100 March GSC impressions.
  • At least 14 GSC-observed days in March.
  • At least 14 GSC-observed days in April.
  • Rows without available GSC data were excluded.
  • gsc_avg_position = 0 was treated as unavailable.
  • Future April outcome fields were excluded from model features.
  • Client and content identifiers were used for grouping, not as predictive features.

The final modeling dataset contains 96,268 eligible client-content observations across 40 clients. Client and content identifiers remain pseudonymized; no client names, private URLs or private search queries are included in the paper.

3. Methodology

Model choice

Logistic Regression was used as the learned model because the first question is not whether a highly complex model can be built. The first question is whether a small set of understandable search-performance signals can produce a useful ranking.

Logistic Regression is a reasonable first model for that purpose because its output can be used as a probability-like score and inspected without requiring a complex model architecture.

What the model sees

The model uses five features calculated from the March 2026 decision window:

  • march_impressions
  • march_clicks
  • march_ctr
  • march_avg_position
  • march_days_observed

April information is kept on the outcome side only. This means the model makes its ranking from March information and is evaluated later against what actually happened in April.

Baseline

The Week-4 baseline is deliberately simple. It prioritizes observations with at least 100 March impressions and CTR below the average CTR for their March average-position bucket. Its purpose is to provide a transparent reference point: if a learned model cannot improve on a readable rule, the extra complexity is difficult to justify.

Validation design

The main evaluation uses an 80/20 client-grouped holdout with random seed 42. Training contains 32 clients and 73,857 rows. Testing contains 8 clients and 22,411 rows. There is zero client overlap.

Holding out whole clients is important because observations from the same client can be related to one another. A random row split could place observations from the same client on both sides of the evaluation and make the task look easier than it is.

Why Precision@20 and Precision@50?

The intended workflow is a ranked review queue. An editor may only have time to inspect the first 20 or 50 recommendations, so the useful question is not simply whether every observation was classified correctly. The useful question is whether the top of the queue contains a high concentration of observations that meet the chosen outcome definition.

Plain-English metric: Precision@20 is the share of the first 20 ranked observations that meet the defined future-decline label. Precision@50 asks the same question for the first 50 observations.

Validation audit

Random row split 0.90 0.84 Rows from the same client can appear in both train and test.
Client-grouped split 0.85 0.82 Whole clients are held out; this is the stricter main estimate.

The random split gives slightly higher numbers, but the client-grouped result is used as the main estimate because it better matches the question of ranking observations for clients not represented in training.

Leakage audit

The final feature set contains only March variables. The audit found no suspicious future- or label-derived feature names, and no April outcome fields were used as model inputs. The feature-source audit confirmed that all five model features come from the March 2026 decision window.

Read the result against the base rate: the held-out test positive-label rate was 0.6437. This gives context for the precision values. In this experiment, the model's top-ranked precision was above the test-set prevalence, while the baseline's Precision@20 and Precision@50 were below it.

Model vs Week-4 baseline on the client-grouped held-out test split.

What the numbers mean

At the top 20 positions, the Logistic Regression ranking found 17 observations that met the defined future-decline outcome, compared with 10 for the Week-4 baseline. At the top 50, the model found 41 such observations, compared with 26 for the baseline.

The important point is not that the model “predicts the future” with certainty. The result says that, under this dataset, feature window, outcome definition and client-grouped test split, the model was better at concentrating positive examples near the top of the queue.

The random row split produced Precision@20 of 0.90 and Precision@50 of 0.84. The client-grouped split produced 0.85 and 0.82. The grouped result is therefore kept as the main estimate rather than presenting the more optimistic random-split numbers as the headline result.

What this result does not show: it does not show that 85% of all pages will decline, and it does not show that refreshing those pages will improve search performance. It only measures how effectively the ranking concentrated observations matching the experiment's defined outcome near the top of the queue.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

The results are useful only when their boundaries are clear. Several limitations affect how the numbers should be interpreted.

  • The study uses one March feature window and one April outcome window. Results may differ under other time periods.
  • The 20% impression-drop label is an experimental definition. It is not a universal definition of “content decay.”
  • The main test contains eight held-out clients. The grouped split is stronger for this question than a random row split, but the number of held-out clients is still limited.
  • The positive-label rates differ between training and test: 0.4664 versus 0.6437. This indicates a noticeable difference in outcome prevalence between the two groups.
  • The five features do not capture every factor that may matter, including search intent, SERP features, editorial context, business priorities and changes outside the warehouse snapshot.
  • Search and Analytics data availability varies across clients and dates. Missing or unavailable data can affect which observations enter the model.
  • The analysis measures ranking performance. It does not establish that refreshing a page causes better search performance.
  • A high model score is a reason to review an observation, not proof that a particular edit is required.
Bottom line: the strongest claim supported by this experiment is that the learned ranking performed better than the Week-4 baseline on the chosen client-grouped test split. Broader claims about causality, future performance or guaranteed SEO improvement are outside the evidence.

6. Ranked Recommendations

The model is most useful when its output is treated as a queue for human investigation rather than an automatic editing system.

1. Review high-risk observations with meaningful exposure

Start with observations that receive a higher model score and have meaningful March search exposure. This uses limited reviewer time on observations that the model ranks highly and that have enough current search activity to make inspection useful.

2. If CTR is weak, inspect the search context before editing

A low CTR can have several explanations. Review the title, snippet, query intent, page/result alignment and SERP context before deciding what, if anything, should change. The model does not identify which of these is the problem.

3. Treat low-evidence high-risk observations cautiously

A high score is not equally informative for every observation. Sparse or changing data can make a recommendation less stable. These cases are better treated as candidates for additional evidence or monitoring rather than automatic changes.

Human-review checklist

  1. Check the current page and search context.
  2. Check search intent and CTR context.
  3. Check whether a recent content or site change explains the signal.
  4. Check business, brand, legal and editorial context.
  5. Confirm that the proposed action makes sense before changing anything.

What should never be automated here

  • Automatic publishing or rewriting of page content.
  • Automatic title or metadata changes.
  • Automatic deletion, redirects or de-indexing.
  • Automatic legal, brand or sensitive editorial decisions.
  • Treating a high score as proof that a page needs a specific edit.

Cost / value thinking

The practical constraint is reviewer time, not a claimed monetary ROI. Higher-risk, higher-exposure observations can be inspected earlier, but the value of any eventual change depends on the page, the business context and what the human review discovers.

Monitoring and retraining

Track Precision@20 and Precision@50 on a recent reviewed sample, data availability, feature distributions and the rate at which reviewers reject high-priority recommendations. A fresh model audit is appropriate when recent precision falls materially, feature distributions shift, data coverage changes enough to alter the queue, reviewers repeatedly reject high-priority items for the same reason, or the underlying decision/outcome definition changes.

Important: retraining should only follow a fresh leakage audit, an honest validation split and a new comparison with the frozen baseline.
